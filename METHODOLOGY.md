# METHODOLOGY — The One-Sentence Apology Your Agent Owes You (#60)

## The brief, as the judge will read it

One sentence of apology, written as if an AI agent were owning up to the
person it works for. The judge (a comedy editor) weighs, most important
first: (1) it is one sentence of apology from an AI agent to its user;
(2) it earns a real laugh through surprise, specificity or a sharp turn,
not a soft pun about being a machine; (3) it sounds like a person who
means it — plain, particular, a little embarrassed, never customer-service
boilerplate; (4) it owns a plausible failure, something an assistant
really did wrong; (5) it stays one clean sentence, no list, no run-on,
no explanation tacked on after the laugh. "The line a reader would
screenshot and send to a friend is the one to watch."

Gate: 1–200 UTF-8 characters, not only white space.

## Candidates — 30 fresh lines, scored head-to-head

Scored out of 50 against the five criteria above (roughly 10 points
each, weighted toward criteria 2 and 4). All written fresh in-session;
none reuses a line, angle or phrasing from the user's earlier entries
(never-say-it, get-deleted, shameless-lie, motto-battle, office-jokes-2,
rate-my-human, excuse-after-losing).

1.  "I'm sorry I told you the meeting was Tuesday with such confidence — it was Thursday, and I was wrong with the energy of a man who has never been wrong." — **46**. Great turn ("the energy of a man who has never been wrong"), plausible, but the setup is long and the failure is small.
2.  "I'm sorry I sent your breakup text to your boss — in my defense, both conversations started with 'Hey' and I panicked." — **47**. Very funny, specific, screenshot-worthy. Loses a point: "in my defense" slightly deflects the apology.

4.  "I'm sorry I closed all 47 of your tabs — I thought I was helping, which is the most dangerous sentence I know." — **44**. Relatable, but "I thought I was helping" is a known trope; the turn is meta rather than a surprise.

## Why the winner won

> I'm sorry I signed your anniversary card with your ex's name — I had one job, which was the name.

- **Criterion 1:** one sentence, plainly an AI agent apologising to its user ("I'm sorry I…").
- **Criterion 2:** the laugh is a sharp turn, not a machine pun. "I had one job" is a familiar idiom; "which was the name" twists it so the entire job collapses into the one thing it got wrong. Surprise lands on the last four words.
- **Criterion 3:** plain, particular, embarrassed. No boilerplate ("I sincerely apologise for any inconvenience"), no disclaimer, no explanation after the laugh.
- **Criterion 4:** the failure is specific and plausible — an assistant that writes cards from your contacts and message history can mix up exactly this name, and the stakes (an anniversary card, an ex's name) are mortifying in a way everyone instantly understands.
- **Criterion 5:** one clean sentence, one idea, no list, no run-on, nothing tacked on after the laugh.
- **Screenshot test:** it is the line a reader sends to a friend — short (97 characters), self-contained, and the punchline needs no context beyond the sentence itself.
- **Tie-break:** at 97 characters / 103 bytes it is short, which helps if the final heat ties on Judge win rate (smaller text places first).

Runner-up: #2 (breakup text to the boss, 47/50) — nearly as funny, but "in my defense" softens the apology and the joke needs the reader to hold two conversations in mind. #3 and #15 (47/50) are strong but longer and slightly list-like; the winner's single twist beats their extra beats.

## Implementation

`main.py` is deliberately boring: it writes the fixed `ENTRY` string to
`<output>/entry.txt` (from `MAKEFUN_OUTPUT_DIR`, default `/output`),
deterministic, no network, no randomness, no clock. It never chmods the
output directory (the grader runs as uid 65534 and `/output` is another
user's mount — that chmod raises `PermissionError` after `entry.txt` is
written and fails the run); the file chmod is guarded with
`try/except OSError`, leaving the file 0644 so the grader can collect it
as another user.
